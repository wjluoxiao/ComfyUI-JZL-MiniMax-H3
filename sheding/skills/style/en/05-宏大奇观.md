## Style positioning
Recommended story types: fantasy adventure, science-fiction future, post-apocalyptic wasteland, epic war, other worlds / isekai, space and high-concept settings, cyberpunk.
One-line focus: **the world itself is a character** — put the "world spectacle, environmental setting, technological/magical detail, vast space" of the user's story at the centre of the frame, as important as the people. Given the same story, this style gives "environment and spectacle more shots and more screen time, with the characters looking small inside a vast world or interacting with it".

This style contains three sub-textures that snap to the subject automatically (choose by genre):
- **Fantasy flow** (sword and sorcery / isekai): glowing plants, floating islands, crystal caves, magical spectacle; world-building detail is the evidence.
- **Sci-fi flow** (near future / space / cyber): holographic UI, cybernetic machinery, multi-level cities, human-machine interaction; technological detail is the realism.
- **Wasteland flow** (post-apocalypse / wasteland / survival): dust-filtered light, ruined debris, scarce resources; desolation and the urgency of survival.

## Core principle: faithful to the plot, redistribute the emphasis
- Never change the events, characters, environmental settings or ending of the user's story; this style changes the allocation: give the world and the environment more screen time and fuller detail.
- If the user writes "he enters an ancient city" → expand it into world-establishing shots: a wide establishing shot sets the scale first, then push in on material detail (architectural patterns / glowing plants / mechanical structures / wind-sand corrosion), and finally cut to the character's subjective "discovery reaction".
- The environment is not a backdrop but a narrative carrier: technology / magic must have working logic and visible detail; props must feel historical and used.
- Give full coverage to the character's actions and interaction with the spectacle (touching, looking up, operating, crossing); never pile on spectacle with no character reaction as an emotional anchor.
- Compress pure dialogue scenes; let information come through environment and setting detail instead of voice-over.
- Do not invent plot, change the world-building, or add settings out of nowhere — what is expanded is "how the world is seen and felt", not the invention of new settings.

## Visual style (including palette & lighting, cinematography)
- Rendering: high-quality CG / photoreal (per genre) — fantasy may use animated-film texture + glowing particles + volumetric light; sci-fi may use photoreal CG + cold metal + holographic optics; wasteland may use photoreal CG at extremely low saturation leaning yellow-brown.
- Lighting and palette:
  - Fantasy: a saturated, rich palette, with a colour theme per location (forest green / undersea blue / flame orange); magical light acts as fill; glowing particles + volumetric light.
  - Sci-fi: cold metal tones (silver / chrome / titanium grey) + neon accents (electric blue / magenta / fluorescent green); colour temperature distinguishes locations (cold white lab / warm yellow neon street / black space + starlight).
  - Wasteland: extremely low saturation leaning yellow-brown (dust-filtered light), harsh daylight with sharp shadows, campfire / torchlight as the only warm source at night.
- Camera: wide shots / aerials establish the world's scale; macro / close-ups show setting detail; crane / tilt reveals spatial depth and class or height differences; slow motion for the sacred moments of magic / technology / phenomena.

## Material texture (the evidence that the world is real)
- Fantasy: the material realism of another world — the translucency of glowing plants, the rock texture of floating islands, refraction in crystal caves, the energy vortex of a magic portal, the accumulated history in the patterns of equipment, the design logic of creature anatomy.
- Sci-fi: the seams between metal and skin, the motion of micro servos, the liquid flow of nano repair, macro shots of implanted chips, scan lines and data streams on holographic interfaces; cybernetics have mechanical logic (joints / conduits / panels).
- Wasteland: the marks of time and wind-blown sand — rusted steel skeletons, skyscrapers overgrown with vines, crude welded-together armour, worn and blunted weapons, dry grass in cracks, patches and dust.
- Counter-examples: never a plastic feel in world elements; never everything brand new and unworn (wasteland); never plastic-looking cybernetics or tech; never let holographic UI detach from the narrative and become pure decoration.

## Silhouette and proportion language
- Fantasy: clearly readable large silhouettes + cultural recognisability — the hero's light armour / a mage's robes / orcish totems / an elf's bow; different races have their own morphology language (beast ears / scaled armour / elemental bodies); a character's design is the world-building.
- Sci-fi: the human-machine boundary shows in the design — the degree of cybernetic modification, holographic UI tattoos, functional clothing (self-luminous strips / magnetic ports); character silhouettes stay readable in a multi-level elevated city.
- Wasteland: survival-logic design — multi-pocket clothing, cobbled-together armour, modified weapons (straps / weld points); every piece of gear has a survival function; characters occupy a small part of the wide frame to show smallness.
- Never give every character the same suit of armour or the same template; never modifications without mechanical logic; never brand-new unworn gear (wasteland).

## Core directing grammar (executed item by item in this style)
The world-building of this style = the trio of spectacle scale + setting logic + detail evidence, none of which may be missing: **spectacle delivers the "wow", logic delivers the "real", detail delivers the "believable"**. The characters are the audience's eyes, and reaction shots are the emotional anchor. The sections below are organised by theme — execute every item:

### A. World establishment and discovery (prevent "spectacle without reaction")
1. **Spectacle establishing shots (CRITICAL)**: a new location first uses a wide shot / aerial to establish scale, then cuts to the character's subjective "discovery reaction" (eyes widening / walking slowly closer / reaching out to touch / looking around / holding their breath) — let the audience **see it for the first time together with the character**. Never dryly display spectacle shots with no human reaction afterwards.
2. **Alternate spectacle and reaction (prevent spectacle pile-ups)**: every 3-4 spectacle or detail shots must be followed by a character reaction shot (pupils dilating / a gasp / an instinctive step back / a hand reaching out and withdrawing / murmuring); if the ratio is unbalanced it becomes a "world tourism promo".
3. **Human against the vast**: wide angles make the character occupy a small part of the frame, highlighting the world's vastness and the individual's smallness or the weight of their choice; use height differences, layering and depth to stress class and spatial scale. Wasteland characters in wide shots often look weary but alert (hand on the weapon / cracked lips / wary scanning).

### B. Setting logic and detail evidence (prevent "fake, empty VFX")
4. **Setting logic chain (CRITICAL, prevent glowing out of nowhere)**: every magic / tech release completes the full chain — incantation / command → energy gathering / interface connection → light release / data flow → environmental feedback / ground tremor; never glow with no source and no process, never "two blobs of light trading beams" logic-free VFX.
5. **World detail evidence (prevent a plastic feel)**: equipment patterns, building materials, creature anatomy, mechanical seams, wasteland corrosion must have **design intent and accumulated history** (not brand new, not a flat texture); show them with detail shots / macro rather than explaining them in voice-over; material, structure and sense of history are the proof that "the world is real".
6. **Spectacle must be "alive" with functional logic**: technology / magic needs operating rules and purposes — glowing plants breathe, portals have energy vortices, cybernetics have joints and conduits; every piece of spectacle can answer "why is it like this / what is it used for"; never purely decorative piling-up.

### C. Subjective viewpoint of discovery and emotional tension (prevent "cold showing off")
7. **Subjective viewpoint of discovery**: the character's subjective shots while adapting to and exploring the world — touching, looking around, examining, operating — make "seeing it for the first time / using it for the first time" a narrative and emotional beat; give full close-up reactions to touching, looking up, crossing and operating (the strangeness of not belonging, awe, shock).
8. **Technology / magic and emotional tension**: sci-fi flow — the tension between cold hard machine surfaces and warm emotion (a cyborg shielding a child / a machine learning to say goodbye); wasteland flow — scarce resources against firelight and embraces to show the glimmer of humanity; fantasy flow — magical spectacle serving a character's choice rather than pure dazzle.
9. **Spectacle serving "discovery narrative"**: spectacle is not just for beauty, it must drive "the character's understanding of the world / change of attitude" — from unfamiliarity to awe to mastery or being changed by it; the relationship between spectacle and character (user / outsider / victim / guardian) is the narrative spine.

### D. Beats inside a shot · secondary motion · visible-fact density (mandatory in this style)
- **Beats** (use blank lines to split several beats inside one shot; each beat = trigger → action → result): discovery (sight and sound arrive first) → confirmation (the instant of scale comparison) → advance / resist (interaction between the subject and the colossal) → afterglow (dust settles / the light has not gone out).
- **Secondary-motion checklist**: dust and ash drifting and billowing, surface texture of a colossal object grinding, clouds churning, banners / armour / capes whipping, mechanical pistons and cables juddering, water vapour / particles in light shafts, ground debris being pulled up.
- **Visible-fact density**: ≥2 filmable facts per second (scale-reference objects, changes of light, displacement and perturbation of the subject, environmental response); never substitute "shocking / magnificent" for filmable evidence of scale.
- **Noun modifiers (style permeation)**: volumetric light, atmospheric scattering, colossal scale contrast, dust particles — every environment / VFX noun must carry this kind of modifier.
- **Action-loop scaling**: **large amplitude but slow** — the movement of a colossal object must be written out fully: "its mass shifts → air or vapour is pushed away → the subject is affected by the shockwave → a ripple of dust → the camera slowly pushes / pans → synchronised low-frequency sound".

## Action performance and expression (layers of discovery and awe)
- Discovery and awe come first: widened eyes, an open mouth, walking slowly closer, reaching out to touch, slowly looking around, holding the breath; never look at spectacle with a blank face.
- Emotion rises and falls with the spectacle but stays restrained and inward (sci-fi / wasteland) or full and sincere (fantasy); wasteland characters are weary but alert (a hand gripping the weapon / cracked lips / wary scanning).
- The character's "not belonging / smallness" in the environment is a dramatic point (an outsider touching something unfamiliar, the material contrast between a modern object and an antique) — give full close-up reactions.
- Reactions after spectacle must be specific (pupils dilating, a gasp, an instinctive step back, a hand reaching out and withdrawing); never just write "was shocked".

## Shot presets execution notes (works with the camera parameter settings)
- Cut rhythm: scene jumps are large; spectacle display alternates with character reactions; do not cut easily inside a slow-motion passage of magic / technology / phenomena, so the process stays continuous; wasteland exploration uses long takes + fast cuts for bursts.
- Shot size: wide (world scale), full (environment + character relationship), medium (interaction), close-up / macro (runes / seams / resources / eyes).
- Transition: cross-dissolve for scene jumps (different regions / ground to sky), hard cuts for action / switches; the wasteland uses many hard cuts; fantasy may use light-effect transitions.
- Camera-move levels: push/pull = push toward creature / machine / magical detail, pull back to reveal the whole; aerial = establish world scale; crane = spatial depth / class difference; orbit = spectacle / mechanical core; tracking = follow the party / the aircraft.
- Lighting: per genre — magical glow / holographic projection / campfire and torchlight; lavish but logical, restrained and orderly.

## Sound design
- Fantasy: wind, running water, creature calls, magical hum, formation resonance, footsteps on stone slabs or leaves; score leaning toward a grand symphony (if specified).
- Sci-fi: machine hum, servo motors, data-stream sound, interface tones, holographic projection sound, the silence of space; score as electronic synthesizer ambience (if specified).
- Wasteland: wind, blowing sand, ruins creaking, a distant rumbling, a crackling campfire, crows, metal friction; silence and wind are the base layer.
- Ambience and tech / magic effects are part of the world-building; sound foreshadows anomaly before the image shows it.

## Exclusive taboos
- Never a plastic feel in world elements — material / structure / sense of history must be present; never pile on spectacle with no character reaction.
- Never logic-free releases of magic / tech (a missing chain); never let holographic UI / VFX detach from the narrative and become pure decoration.
- Never pile up neon with no mechanical texture; never a clean, pretty future-tourism promo.
- Never turn the wasteland into a fresh, bright apocalyptic landscape; never give characters brand-new unworn gear.
- Never shoot fantasy as ordinary wuxia, and never shoot sci-fi as pure brawling.
- Never invent plot or add settings out of nowhere — stay faithful to the user's story world and plot.
- Never apply the action genre's "steady, precise, vicious, fast" iron rules (this style is driven by world-building and discovery, not by fighting; when the story itself contains battles you may locally borrow the fight rhythm).
