## Style positioning
Recommended story types: mystery deduction, thriller / horror, espionage shadow war, film noir, psychological games, court intrigue, criminal investigation.
One-line focus: **suspense and unease are the soul of this style** — amplify the "information gap, concealment, foreshadowing, pressure" in the user's story into the body of the shot. Horror does not rely on gore, espionage does not rely on gunfights, intrigue does not rely on brawls; everything depends on "the audience knows and the character doesn't" and "someone is hiding in the dark" anticipatory tension.

This style contains four sub-textures that snap to the subject automatically (choose by genre):
- **Deduction clue flow** (detective / mystery / investigation): physical-evidence detail, micro-expressions, gradual disclosure; information asymmetry is the engine.
- **Psychological thriller flow** (horror / thriller): a release-hold-release breath; the monster is heard before it is seen; silence lasts longer than the scare.
- **Espionage shadow-war flow** (spy / infiltration / assassination): codes / tokens / being watched; a single glance or handover is a matter of life and death.
- **Noir fatalism flow** (film noir / intrigue): the boundary between light and dark, inner monologue, moral struggle, fatalism and calculation.

## Core principle: faithful to the plot, redistribute the emphasis
- Never change the events, characters, truth or ending of the user's story; this style changes **how much the camera lets the audience see, when they see it, and how long it lingers**.
- Use "reveal and conceal" as the rhythm engine: distribute key clues / identities / truths as "slow accumulation → key reveal → brief release → accumulate again"; the truth is shaken out over several beats, never told in one go.
- Amplify "process": handovers, searching, spying, tailing, inner struggle and other procedural content take most of the screen time; compress plain exposition and explanation.
- Do not invent plot, change the truth, or force in a twist — only strengthen "narrative tension and the way information is presented".
- If the user provides dialogue, keep its meaning; hide key information in details and reactions, and avoid long speeches that directly explain deductions or schemes.

## Visual style (including palette & lighting, cinematography)
- Rendering: realistic cinematic; desaturated cool tones (blue-grey / dark green / dark brown) or retro monochrome / black and white; shallow depth of field, telephoto compression of space; low exposure for a murky, filtered feel.
- Lighting: a single key source (desk lamp / flashlight / neon / street lamp / candlelight) creates strong chiaroscuro; shadow is where information hides and becomes the subject of composition; characters are half hidden, showing only one eye / half a face / one trembling hand.
- Materials and sets: traces of time on old objects (fibres in a creased letter, peeling paint on a half-open door, muddy footprints on the floor, fingerprints reflected on evidence, a lipstick mark on a teacup); horror flow uses stale, damp textures (mottled walls / water stains and mould / peeling plaster); espionage flow uses period textures (qipao silk / telegraph machine / creases in a coded letter). Props must survive frame-by-frame scrutiny — material detail is the clue.
- Camera: locked-off or extremely slow dolly so the audience can scan for information; Dutch angles signal unease; voyeuristic framing (door crack / window frame / branches / blinds as foreground occlusion) creates the feeling of "being watched / peeping"; shallow depth of field locks attention onto the key object.

## Silhouette and proportion language
- Identity hides in detail: a ring, a scar, an ill-fitting coat, a pair of worn shoes — the audience assembles the puzzle from details.
- Characters are half hidden: the detective's exhaustion (messy hair / rumpled clothes), the suspect's evasiveness (shifting eyes / curling fingertips), the informant's wariness, the hard-boiled trench coat and fedora, the schemer's robes and bearing (status is costume).
- Never explain identity through voice-over; identity is carried by visual detail and bearing.

## Core directing grammar (executed item by item in this style)
The engine of suspense = information gap (the audience and the character know different things) + pressure rhythm (hold and release / delay); both are controlled quantitatively. The sections below are organised by theme — execute every item:

### A. Suspense engine (prevent "spoiler-style flat telling")
1. **Information asymmetry is the default camera logic**: the camera always shows the audience either a little less or a little more than "the character knows" — alternate "the audience knows, the character doesn't" (sweating for the character) with "the character knows, the audience doesn't" (waiting for the character to break); never tell everything from a fully omniscient flat perspective.
2. **Reveal rhythm (prevent telling it all at once)**: truth / identity cycle through "slow accumulation → key reveal → brief release → accumulate again", **shaken out over several beats**; each reveal advances only one layer and never goes all the way, with at least 1 s of silence before and after (empty shot / micro-expression / ambience) to build pressure; reveal with a push-in or a close-up, never a jump cut. The key answer always waits for the story position where it belongs; never spoil it early.
3. **Expectation management (prevent instant answers)**: once the audience's question is raised, "give a little, take a little" — insert a distraction thread / false answer / detour then pull back; push the "answer" later and push the "sense of closing in" earlier (approaching footsteps, searching hands, a closing deadline / clock).
4. **Make the MacGuffin visible**: the token / object / information that triggers the chase must **reappear in close-up repeatedly, each time adding a new detail** (crease / fingerprint / serial number / stain) so the audience can assemble the puzzle alongside the characters; never mention it only in dialogue with no visual carrier.
5. **Identity hides in detail**: identities / relationships are not declared in dialogue but pieced together from a ring, a scar, an ill-fitting coat, worn shoes, a half-hidden letter; every key prop must survive frame-by-frame scrutiny — material detail is the clue.

### B. Pressure and thriller rhythm (prevent "pile-ups of jump scares")
6. **Release-hold-release breathing (CRITICAL, psychological thriller flow)**: silent passages must be **longer** than scare passages (roughly 2:1 or more) — the audience frightens itself while waiting; rhythm = stretch the silence → a shadow stirs / an odd sound → sudden eruption → back to even longer silence; at least 2-3 hold-release cycles per scene; no consecutive scares (they numb the audience and look cheap).
7. **The monster is heard before it is seen**: sound / shadow / door crack / an extremely brief flash (≤0.3 s) first establish "something is there"; the full form / face is saved for the climax; never reveal the whole thing in the middle, never build horror on gore.
8. **Sound before image (CRITICAL)**: danger is **always heard half a beat before it enters frame** (footsteps before the figure, the hinge before the door opens, breathing before the turn); the audio-visual gap (sudden silence) works better than a scream; use room tone (rain / ticking / low hum / electrical buzz) to keep the tension from breaking.
9. **Oppressive composition**: Dutch angles signal unease; place the character at the edge of frame and hand large areas to shadow / empty space to create "a space something is about to come into"; low angles closing in and extremely slow dollies all serve one goal — making the audience feel watched, cornered, with nowhere to run.

### C. Voyeurism and shadow-war vocabulary (espionage / intrigue / noir flow)
10. **Occluded peeping**: door frames / blinds / branches / furniture as foreground occlusion create the unease of "someone watching from the dark / being watched right now"; tailing and being tailed use shallow depth of field and telephoto compression to simulate the feeling of surveillance.
11. **Codes and handovers (espionage flow)**: contact tokens (a torn banknote / a book / a ring), creases in a coded letter, hand-signal codes get full close-ups so the audience can identify them; the handover action is **one touch and apart, the gaze unchanged, the surroundings flowing normally** — the most dangerous moment looks the most ordinary.
12. **Struggle on the light-dark boundary (noir / intrigue flow)**: characters stand on the border of light and dark, striped shadows (blinds / railings) slice across the face, visualising inner division and moral struggle; symmetrical / asymmetrical composition implies power relations; monologue with an extremely slow push-in approaches the secret; elongated neon / street-lamp reflections on wet ground reinforce fatalism.

### D. Beats inside a shot · secondary motion · visible-fact density (mandatory in this style)
- **Beats** (use blank lines to split several beats inside one shot; each beat = trigger → action → result): notice the anomaly (the eyes and breath move first) → hold the breath and confirm (micro-action) → probe and approach (lean forward / reach out / step) → the truth or the scare lands (or a deliberate blank).
- **Secondary-motion checklist**: a curtain lifted by a draught, a narrow patch of light moving through a door crack / blinds, a torch beam sweeping the wall, breath vapour, ripples in a teacup, the corner of a page turning, a shadow stretched and crawling by the light source, dust drifting in a light shaft.
- **Visible-fact density**: ≥2 filmable facts per second, but **the facts are mostly micro-actions** (fingertips, Adam's apple, pupils, the rise and fall of breath, a moving light patch, a shadow deforming) — never substitute words like "the atmosphere is oppressive / tense".
- **Noun modifiers (style permeation)**: cool blue-grey tone, low-key hard-edged shadow, damp reflections, mist / dust particles — every environment / prop noun must carry this kind of modifier.
- **Action-loop scaling**: **small amplitude** — even a single reach must be written out fully: "fingertips lift → contact point → the other's muscles tighten / half a step back → the shadow is disturbed → the camera pushes in slightly → synchronised sound".

## Action performance and expression (restraint is the core)
- Actions are restrained, hesitant, wary: a slowed head turn, held breath, a hand that hovers and then withdraws; never exaggerated and outgoing.
- Emotion is kept in detail: a hand that clenches and releases, evasive eyes, a crumpled corner of paper, a fine sweat on the temple, a bobbing Adam's apple.
- In silence, micro-movement is more lethal than big action (espionage / intrigue); hesitation means concealment, a pause means calculation.
- The thriller flow's "ruled by fear": a stiff turn, trembling fingertips, rapid breathing are part of the performance.
- Relationships / power relations are conveyed through bearing: the depth of a bow, seating order, eye contact, lowered eyes.

## Shot presets execution notes (works with the camera parameter settings)
- Cut rhythm: slower than an action film; hold each information point long enough before cutting; keep 1 s of silence before and after a key reveal (empty shot / micro-expression); reveal with a push-in or close-up rather than a jump cut; in thriller silent passages do not cut easily, and cut after a scare erupts.
- Shot size: medium-close (standoff / interrogation), close-up (clue / micro-expression / trembling hand / token), long shot (the loneliness of being watched).
- Transition: cross-dissolve for time / space jumps, hard cut at the instant of a reveal; the thriller flow may use black screen + sound effect to jolt the audience.
- Camera-move levels: push/pull = extremely slow push toward the evidence / eyes / token; pan/truck = slowly sweep the scene; tracking = tailing; orbit = the two sides in a standoff; crane = architectural pressure / power hierarchy; rotation = reveal spatial relations.
- Lighting: single key source with long shadows, cool neon / street light, torch / candle, blind stripes; keep lighting restrained — shadow is the narration. Never bright, even lighting.

## Sound design
- Room tone: rain, ticking clock, the creak of an old building, a distant low hum, telegraph ticks, electrical buzz, breathing in silence.
- Key effects: approaching footsteps, sudden silence, paper friction, coded knocks, a heartbeat, a coded letter being folded, a whispered handover, a sudden gunshot.
- Silence and whispers are the soul of this style's sound; sound reveals danger before the image; never a cheerful score feel.
- Thriller flow: odd sounds / whispers / sudden silence are the first layer of horror; espionage flow: telegraph, footsteps and rain carry the tension.

## Exclusive taboos
- Never reveal the answer / truth too early (save it for the end); never let a clue flash by too fast for the audience to read.
- Never explain deduction or scheming in long speeches — show it with visual detail; never explain identity through voice-over.
- Never turn a thriller into gore and violence, never turn espionage into a gun battle, never turn intrigue into martial arts.
- Never use bright even lighting; never let characters emote in an exaggerated, outgoing way; never let actions be big and sweeping (it destroys the restraint).
- Never invent a truth the user's story does not contain just to create a "twist" — stay faithful to the original story's information structure.
- Never apply the action genre's "steady, precise, vicious, fast" iron rules or its fast cutting / large dynamic camera moves.
